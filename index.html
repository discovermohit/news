Parliament tables a Vande Mataram Bill — then adjourns till Monday

One concrete piece of legislation squeezed through the noise before the shutters came down. The government moved a Bill in the Rajya Sabha to give the national song "Vande Mataram" legal protection under the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 — placing it on the same statutory footing as the national anthem — but within minutes of convening, both the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha were adjourned till Monday amid uproar over the crackdown on the Opposition's 'Chalo Sansad' protest march. A whole working week of the Monsoon Session, gone to procedure.

  • Vande Mataram Bill tabled by MoS Home Nityanand Rai.
  • It amends the 1971 National Honour Act to shield the song.
  • Both Houses adjourned till Monday after brief sittings.
  • Flashpoint: the 'Chalo Sansad' protest-march crackdown.
Why it matters:Pushing a symbolic-nationalism bill through on a day the rest of the agenda collapses is a familiar pattern — the emotive legislation advances while the contested tax, judicial and election bills pile up for the session's final week, when they'll be rushed through with the least scrutiny and the lowest public attention.

The Centre moves to add four Supreme Court judges

Amid the chaos, one concrete piece of business is inching forward. Law minister Arjun Ram Meghwal is set to move the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026, which raises the court's sanctioned strength from 33 judges to 37 — formalising an ordinance the Centre had already promulgated to ease a mounting case backlog. Four extra seats on the country's top bench is a bigger deal than the sleepy bill title suggests.

  • Bill raises Supreme Court strength from 33 to 37 judges.
  • Moved by law minister Arjun Ram Meghwal.
  • It replaces an ordinance already promulgated by the Centre.
  • Aim: help clear the court's chronic case backlog.
Why it matters:Expanding the top court sounds administrative, but who fills those four seats — and how fast — shapes the judiciary's capacity to hear everything from constitutional challenges to commercial disputes, so the fight worth watching isn't the headcount but the appointments that follow it.

A bill to grow the Lok Sabha to 850 seats is back on the table

One of the most consequential structural reforms in Indian politics is quietly re-entering the frame. The Centre is expected to make another attempt at the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, which would expand the Lok Sabha to 850 members and reserve 33% of its seats for women — a change that would reshape both the size and the composition of India's lower house. It is the kind of reform that outlives whichever session it passes in.

  • Bill would enlarge the Lok Sabha to 850 members.
  • It ties in 33% reservation for women.
  • The Centre is making another attempt at the amendment.
  • It would reset the size and make-up of the lower house.
Why it matters:Expanding the Lok Sabha revives the long-deferred delimitation question — more seats means redrawing constituency boundaries, and any redraw shifts power toward the faster-growing northern states, which is precisely why this "reform" is really a fight over regional political weight dressed up as women's representation.

Gartner: $2.59 trillion of AI spend this year — and 45% of it is plumbing

The headline number is enormous; the composition is the actual story. Gartner puts worldwide AI spending at $2.59 trillion in 2026, up 47% year-on-year — but more than 45% of that goes to infrastructure: AI-optimised servers, network fabric, processing semiconductors and IaaS. Nearly half the AI boom is, in accounting terms, real estate and electricity.

  • Worldwide AI spend: $2.59 trillion in 2026, up 47%.
  • Over 45% of it is AI infrastructure, not software.
  • Covers AI-optimised IaaS, servers, network fabric, semiconductors.
  • Gartner's January estimate was $2.5tn — it has been revised up since.
Why it matters:If nearly half of AI spending is capex on servers and fabric, then the "AI market" is mostly a hardware cycle wearing a software costume — and hardware cycles turn, which is why the software and services layer is racing to attach itself to something recurring before the build-out plateaus.

The $2.5tn vs $487bn trap — the most common budgeting error of the year

Two respectable analyst numbers, wildly different, and people keep putting them in the same slide. Gartner's headline counts total AI spending — infrastructure, services, software, devices — while IDC's $487bn figure for 2026 measures AI infrastructure hardware only, which means the two forecasts are not alternatives to each other and mixing them is, as one analysis puts it, the single most common analytical error in technology budgeting. Read the denominator before you quote the number.

  • Gartner: $2.59tn — the whole AI stack, end to end.
  • IDC: $487bn — AI infrastructure hardware only for 2026.
  • The forecasts measure fundamentally different things.
  • Mixing them is flagged as the year's most common budgeting error.
Why it matters:Every board deck this quarter will cite one of these numbers to justify a budget — and the person who knows which scope each figure covers wins the argument, because a 5x discrepancy in a headline is usually a definitional gap, not a disagreement about the future.
